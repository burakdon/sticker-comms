# Presentation outline — "What does this sticker actually mean?"

8 minutes max, in class Nov 3. 14 slides, about 30 s each; the results section gets the most time.
Speakers alternate: **B** = Burak, **C** = Canyang.

**Look:** same family as the Big Olive deck. Off-white background (`FBFBF9`), slate text (`3A4048`), Inter headlines at silly sizes, one accent. For this deck the accent is **WhatsApp green (`25D366`)** instead of olive gold, and the visual motif is **chat bubbles**: every quote, stat and caption sits in a bubble. Real stickers from the study float around as decoration (the spares, never the study set, until the results slides).

## Act 2 — the question (1:15)

### 4. "Our group chat runs on these" (0:30) — B
**Visual:** three real screenshots from the group chat, non-Turkish members reacting to Turkish-politician stickers with 😂. Faces blurred, names cropped.
**Beat:** "Half the chat is Turkish. The other half laughs at Bülent Arınç crying. Do they *get* it, or are they being polite?" Motivation in one breath.

### 5. "Can you read a sticker from a culture you're not from?" (0:45) — C
**Visual:** three chat bubbles, one per hypothesis, in plain language:
- 💬 Does seeing the sticker **inside a chat** help? *(H1, the randomized one)*
- 🏠 Do natives read their own stickers better? *(H2)*
- 👀 Can outsiders beat chance with **no context at all**? *(H4)*
Plus a tiny fourth bubble: "and is it the sticker, or just the message?" *(H5, the text-only control)*.
**Beat:** name the literature in one phrase, "the in-group advantage in emotion recognition (Elfenbein & Ambady 2002), but for stickers."

## Act 3 — the experiment (2:00)

### 6. Live round: "Guess the sentiment" (0:50) — B
**Visual:** one sticker shown alone, large. Class votes by hand: positive / negative / sarcastic. Click: same sticker inside the chat mockup with its message. Vote again. Do it with **Tatlıses + gun** (16% of outsiders got it alone) and **the Chinese "I'm fine" lying in the water** (30%).
**Beat:** the class experiences the manipulation before seeing the data. Reveal the answer key after each. This is the slide people remember.

### 7. "36 stickers, 3 cultures, 3 ways to see them" (0:40) — C
**Visual:** a 3 × 3 grid: rows = Turkish / Chinese / English (one example sticker each), columns = **alone / in chat / message only** (the actual task screenshots). Below, three small bubbles: *6 with text + 6 without per culture · every sticker once per person · Latin square so every sticker gets every condition.*
**Beat:** one sentence on the design: within-subject, the context condition is randomized, native-or-not is measured. Mention the answer key is **sender intent**: the author of each set labeled what *they* mean.

### 8. "How many people did we need?" (0:30) — C
**Visual:** the power curve figure (`outputs/figures/power_curve.png`), cropped to the simulated panel, with a big stat bubble: **"52 planned → 70 collected."**
**Beat:** paired t-test, α = .05, 80% power, d_z = 0.4 → 52. Simulation of the real trial structure agreed. We beat it. One breath.

## Act 4 — results (2:20)

### 9. "Context helps. +11 points." (0:40) — B
**Visual:** Figure 1 as a pictogram in the Big Olive style: three rows (alone / in chat / message only), ten sticker-shaped icons each, 6.4 / 7.6 / 6.8 filled green. Big numbers on the left: **64% · 76% · 68%**.
**Beat:** H1 confirmed: chat > alone, d_z = 0.58, p < .0001. H5 too: chat > message-only by 7.5 points, so the sticker adds meaning beyond the words. Say "95% CI 6.6 to 16" once, out loud, for the grader.

### 10. "Natives win… until you hide the sticker" (0:40) — C
**Visual:** Figure 2, the two-line interaction plot, with two bubbles pointing at it: *"in-group +51% odds"* on the gap at the left, *"lines meet here"* at message-only.
**Beat:** H2 confirmed. H3 was *not* what we predicted: context helped insiders and outsiders about the same. The interaction that did show up: hide the sticker and natives lose their edge entirely. "Natives are reading the picture, not the caption."

### 11. "Outsiders are not guessing" (0:20) — B
**Visual:** a single giant **67%** with a faint dashed line at 33% labeled "chance". Below, the three hardest stickers for outsiders as small images: Tatlıses (16%), Chinese "I'm fine" (30%), Bahçeli (43%).
**Beat:** H4: on no-text stickers with no context, outsiders scored 67% against a 33% chance rate, p < .0001. Sentiment leaks through the image. Except when a man points a gun at you as a joke.

### 12. "Sarcasm is where the authors and their own people part ways" (0:40) — C
**Visual:** Figure 4 cropped to the bottom six bars (the flagged stickers), each with its sticker image: Hasbulla crying, Pedro the monkey, SİKİN, napim, the two Chinese "I'm fine" ones. Caption bubble: *"6 of 36 stickers: fewer than half of natives agreed with the author. All six are sarcastic or negative."*
**Beat:** the pre-registered answer-key check. We wrote down "what I mean when I send this"; our own communities disagreed on exactly the sarcastic ones. Results hold with these removed (say so). This is the honest, slightly embarrassing slide and it plays well.

## Act 5 — close (1:15)

### 13. "What the data cannot tell us" (0:35) — B
**Visual:** same layout as the olive deck's limitations slide, four short bubbles:
- *Convenience sample:* friends, classmates, WeChat. Young and online.
- *Native-or-not was measured, not randomized.* Quasi-experimental for that factor.
- *The answer key is one person's intent per culture.* Sarcasm is inherently contested.
- *Our attention check was itself ambiguous* ("select POSITIVE" vs "what does the sender mean?"). We kept the 8 "failures" after showing they behaved like everyone else; the pre-registered exclusion gives the same conclusions.

### 14. Takeaway + the squishy draw (0:40) — both
**Visual:** left half, the takeaway in one bubble: **"The sticker carries the meaning. Context helps everyone, but the picture is what natives are reading."** Right half: the baby squishy photo and a big **"Draw time 🧸"**.
**Beat:** one-sentence takeaway, then run the giveaway live: `python3 -c "import random, pandas as pd; print(random.choice(pd.read_csv('data/private/giveaway.csv').name.tolist()))"` on screen, or a pre-rolled name. Two winners: one from everyone, one reserved for AIPI students in the room. Hand over the squishy.
**Closer (footnote style, last click):** `*Some say the hardest sticker in the study was sent by Burak's uncle. Some: Burak.`
