01 / THE QUESTION

Same sticker.
Different conversation.

A sticker is a tiny message with a lot left unsaid.

A smile can be friendly. It can also be sarcastic. Cultural knowledge may help you read it—but so might the message that came just before.

Research on emotion recognition suggests an in-group advantage: people can be better at recognizing emotions within their own cultural group. We asked how that idea plays out in everyday chat stickers. [1]

Does chat context help people recognize a sticker’s intended sentiment?

Here, “correct” means agreement with the author’s intended label: positive, negative, or sarcastic. It does not mean there is only one reasonable interpretation.

ABSTRACT
THE STUDY, IN BRIEF

Seventy participants interpreted stickers from Chinese, Turkish, and English/American sets under three presentation conditions. Chat context increased mean accuracy over a sticker alone by 11.3 percentage points. Showing the sticker also added value beyond the chat line itself. Cultural familiarity mattered, but the evidence did not clearly show a larger chat benefit for outsiders.

A fairer comparison

A Latin-square rotation assigned three balanced sticker sets to the three conditions. Trial order was shuffled. Rotation was random; observed group sizes were 27, 18, and 25, so counterbalancing was approximate rather than exact.

One person, three conditions

We compared each person’s condition-level accuracy using paired tests. This reduces the influence of stable differences between people. Familiarity was self-reported—native, some, or none—not randomly assigned.

Power before precision

The planned paired test needed 52 people for 80% power at an assumed dz = 0.40 and two-sided α = .05. Our snapshot has 70; that does not guarantee power for small effects or cultural interactions.

Assumptions, uncertainty & multiple comparisons +

Shapiro–Wilk p = .294 and .655 for the H1/H5 paired differences; these tests did not identify strong non-normality. Wilcoxon checks agreed (p = .0000388 and .00295). Context RM-ANOVA: F(2,138) = 10.82, p = .0000432, partial η² = .136. Mauchly p = .178; Greenhouse–Geisser p = .0000605.

Holm-corrected post hoc p-values: alone/chat .0000245; chat/text-only .00374; alone/text-only .169. The correction applies to these three comparisons, not every hypothesis in the project. No clear difference was found between alone and text-only.

AND CULTURE?

Familiarity helped.
A bigger outsider benefit?
Not clearly.

In the sticker-alone reference condition, native familiarity was associated with higher odds of matching the intended label: OR 1.51, approximate 95% posterior interval 1.26–1.81.

The chat × in-group interaction was OR 1.32 [0.92–1.90]. Its uncertainty includes no interaction. We cannot claim that chat helped outsiders more—or that the two groups benefited equally.

A variational-Bayes logistic model accounted for repeated participants and stickers using crossed random intercepts (1,543 in/out-group trials). “Some” familiarity was excluded from this model. These are approximate posterior intervals, not frequentist confidence intervals. Model output ↗

A decision worth making visible.

The original plan called for attention and flagged-sticker exclusions. The main analysis retains both; the checks above apply each exclusion separately. They do not test both together. We report this deviation rather than calling the main analysis fully preregistered.

Evidence: attention-exclusion results · sticker-exclusion results · answer-key agreement

What about above-chance recognition (H4)? +

The original H4 concerned Turkish and Chinese non-text stickers. The implemented script also includes the English/American set, so we label its result as a broader exploratory analysis, not a completed test of the narrower original hypothesis.

On 276 out-group, non-text, visible-sticker trials, accuracy was 67.0% versus chance 33.3%; the one-sided exact binomial p = 3.38 × 10⁻³⁰ and one-sided 95% interval is [62.1%, 100%], Cohen’s h = .687. A participant-level check gives 65.9% for 55 participants, t(54) = 11.45, one-sided p = 2.27 × 10⁻¹⁶.

The pooled binomial test ignores clustering; the participant-level check still shares stickers across people. These limits prevent treating the tiny p-value as definitive evidence of universal cross-cultural decoding.

05 / THE MEANING

More context.
Less guesswork.
Still not mind-reading.

For these people and these stickers, the conversation helped.

That is our strongest conclusion. Chat outperformed both a sticker alone and the chat line alone. The effect survived two separate sensitivity checks.

The next step is a larger, more balanced replication with independently validated labels, measured language proficiency, and exclusion rules fixed before collecting responses.

A sticker carries a message.
The conversation helps us read it.✓✓

Where the claim stops

  1. A convenience sample. Friends and personal networks are not representative populations. Native-familiarity counts overlap: 16 Turkish, 26 Chinese, 21 American.
  2. Intent is an answer key, not truth. Insiders disagreed on six stimuli. Sarcasm and sentiment boundaries are not universal.
  3. English is part of the context. The lines may favor English-proficient participants. Familiarity is not nationality.
  4. Only 36 selected stickers. The results do not describe every sticker or every cultural group.
  5. Analysis choices matter. Exclusion deviations, H4 scope, and model approximations are disclosed. A non-significant interaction does not prove equality.
  6. Access and timing can select the sample. Browser/network barriers and response-time cutoffs may exclude otherwise valid responses.
